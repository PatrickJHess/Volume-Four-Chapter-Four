🛡️ Beyond Duration & Convexity: Simulating Risk to Drive Strategy


🚀 Launch Live in Binder ⬅️ Return to Main Book

There are two important related facts that need to be accounted for by any strategy that controls the interest rate risk of a portfolio or attempts to develop an investment strategy based upon investors’ beliefs about potential changes in the term structure.

Duration and Convexity: These metrics are not fixed but change with changes in interest rates. Both measures are derivatives of the present value formula, and those derivatives are not constant. The last time you took a flight, you took off, flew at roughly a constant altitude, and landed. The speed, angle of approach, and altitude varied in each of those stages, and the pilots adjusted their actions according to the stage of the flight. Portfolio managers do the exact same thing when reacting to changes in interest rates.

Non-Parallel Yield Curve Shifts: As demonstrated in Chapter Three, changes in yields alter the shape of the term structure. For example, the inverted case of Chapter Three caused the yield to maturity of a one-year bond to increase by 82 basis points, while the twenty-nine-plus year bond decreased by 122 basis points. Understanding the state of the economy, and therefore the expected shape of the term structure, is crucial for managing interest rate risk

🧠Simulation of the Term Structure that Incorporate Beliefs¶

Portfolio managers don’t live in a vacuum. They are required to have a strong knowledge of the current state of the economy and financial markets and how risks might be expressed. The examples we rely on here are FED policies. Two opposite strategies are considered with unsurprisingly conflicting portfolio management implications. Speed is good when the plane is cruising but not so when it’s landing.

🎲Setting Bounds and Loading the Dice¶

The first place a portfolio manager has to come to is what he thinks is possible. If it’s a cut in the short rate, how big or small could it be? Beliefs about quantitative tightening or easing have to be expressed in some tractable way. Like the pilot adjusting to the state of the flight, the portfolio manager needs to understand what’s likely to happen next.

To put beliefs into practice, probabilities have to be assigned to the ranges of values. For that the portfolio managers need an intuitive way to convert beliefs into a mathematical relation-not something that is written in stone, but provides a method of quantifying and understanding what matters and what doesn’t. The pilot needs instruments, the portfolio needs a recipe.

These needs can be met with the Beta distribution. It’s nothing like the beta that you hear about everyday, but a very convenient and intuitive way to express beliefs in a quantitative way.

The Beta distribution lets us control both the minimum and maximum values and how the probability is allocated between those values. There are two parameters that control the allocation of probability: α and β\alpha \text{ and } \betaα and β. These parameters do two things:

  • ⚖️ Skew or Tilt of the distribution:

    • if α>β\alpha > \betaα>β the distribution is more concentrated to the right tilted towards higher values.

    • if α<β\alpha < \betaα<β the distribution is more concentrated to the left tilted towards lower values.

    • if α=β\alpha = \betaα=β the distribution is centered and not tilted towards higher or lower values.

  • 🎯 Tightness or Conviction About Values:

    • If α=β=1\alpha = \beta =1α=β=1 you have no conviction about any of the possible values. The probability of a value between any two values only depends upon the distance between the values and not their respective values. It’s called a uniform distribution.

    • If α=β=2\alpha = \beta = 2α=β=2 you have a belief that values in and around what you expect are likely, but the conviction isn’t strong. The probability of values away from the expected are substantial. A bell shaped curve with plenty of area away from what expect.

    • If α=β=20\alpha = \beta = 20α=β=20 you have a strong belief that values will be close to your expectation. Large deviations are unlikely. A bell shaped curve with a small area away from what you expect.

Note

: Bear in mind that both the tilt and conviction are at work for any pair of α and β\alpha \text{ and } \betaα and β.

🧩 The Cohesion Problem: Preventing Impossible Outcomes¶

Once a portfolio manager has customized their individual beliefs for Level, SOFR, and Curvature using Beta distributions, a major hazard emerges. If you pull random values from each of these three distributions independently, you are highly likely to create impossible market outcomes.

For instance, your simulation might pair an extremely high overall interest rate Level with a massive, steeply upward-sloping curve. Perhaps a useful novel thought experiment, but completely useless as a simulation to inform an actual investment strategy. In the real world, macro forces lock these variables together; when overall rate levels spike, the yield curve historically tends to flatten or invert.

If our simulation doesn’t respect these fundamental market relationships, our portfolio stress tests will generate meaningless data. The pilot cannot adjust the plane’s altitude without affecting its airspeed and drag; a portfolio manager cannot shift the level of the yield curve without affecting its slope.

To prevent these impossible outcomes, we need a mathematical tool that acts like connecting tissue, binding our variables together without crushing the unique individual shapes (the skews and tightness) representing our beliefs.

That tool is the Copula.

🧬 Completing the Uncertainty: Tying the Shapes Together¶

The word copula comes from the Latin word for “link” or “tie” (the same root as the English word couple). In quantitative finance, a copula does something incredibly elegant: it splits a market scenario’s DNA into two completely independent parts:

  1. The Marginal Beta Distributions (Beliefs): 🧠 The individual, subjective beliefs about the boundaries, tilt, and tightness for Level, SOFR, and Curvature.

  2. The Dependence Structure (How Beliefs Relate): 📊 The historical, statistical reality of how these three factors move together.

⚙️ How the Copula Engine Works¶

Instead of drawing numbers directly from the custom Beta distributions, the simulation performs a multi-step sequence behind the scenes:

  1. Draw Correlated Data: 🎲 The engine first draws random numbers from a standard joint framework (a Multivariate Normal distribution) that is strictly governed by the market’s historical correlation matrix.

  2. Extract Pure Probability:💧It converts those raw draws into pure, correlated probabilities between 0 and 1. If the model draws a high probability for a rising Level, the correlation matrix forces it to simultaneously draw a matching probability for a high SOFR rate (a flattening slope).

  3. Map to Your Beliefs: 🗺️ Finally, it passes those correlated probabilities into your custom Beta distributions anchoring beliefs to market realities.

The beauty of this architecture is that the Copula perfectly preserves the real-world correlation structure between Level, SOFR, and Curvature, yet allowing the final values to morph into the exact left-skewed, right-skewed, or ultra-tight shapes specified by the beliefs. It guarantees that every one of the simulations is grounded in reality. An authentic experiment in how a portfolio’s duration and convexity truly behaves.

📈 Going From the Correlation of Level,Slope, and Curvature to the SOFR Covariance Matrix¶

To execute a realistic simulation of the term structure using the Nelson-Siegel framework, we must establish how the underlying factors relate to one another. We begin with the foundational empirical estimates from the landmark paper by Diebold and Li, which find a historical correlation between the Level (β0\beta_0β0​) and Slope (β1\beta_1β1​) factors of approximately -0.4.

To isolate the impact of policy changes, we treat the short rate r(0)r(0)r(0)—which asymptotically acts as our SOFR proxy—as our primary innovation. Under the Nelson-Siegel framework, the short rate is explicitly defined by the structural identity:

r(0)=β0+β1r(0) = \beta_0 + \beta_1r(0)=β0​+β1​
(1)

Assuming normalized baseline variances for our Level and Slope parameters such that σβ02=1\sigma_{\beta_0}^{2} = 1σβ0​2​=1 and σβ12=1\sigma_{\beta_1}^{2} = 1σβ1​2​=1, we can use standard linear combinations of random variables to derive the implied variance of our short rate innovation and its correlation with the overall curve Level.

The variance of the sum of two correlated variables is governed by their individual variances and their covariance:

σr(0)2=σβ02+σβ12+2ρβ0,β1σβ0σβ1\sigma_{r(0)}^{2} = \sigma_{\beta_0}^{2} + \sigma_{\beta_1}^{2} + 2\rho_{\beta_0,\beta_1}\sigma_{\beta_0}\sigma_{\beta_1}σr(0)2​=σβ0​2​+σβ1​2​+2ρβ0​,β1​​σβ0​​σβ1​​

σr(0)2=1+1+2(−0.4)(1)(1)=1.2\sigma_{r(0)}^{2} = 1 + 1 + 2(-0.4)(1)(1) = 1.2σr(0)2​=1+1+2(−0.4)(1)(1)=1.2

σr(0),β0=Cov(β0+β1,β0)=σβ02+ρβ0,β1σβ0σβ1\sigma_{r(0),\beta_0} = \text{Cov}(\beta_0 + \beta_1, \beta_0) = \sigma_{\beta_0}^{2} + \rho_{\beta_0,\beta_1}\sigma_{\beta_0}\sigma_{\beta_1}σr(0),β0​​=Cov(β0​+β1​,β0​)=σβ0​2​+ρβ0​,β1​​σβ0​​σβ1​​

4.🗂️ The Covariance Matrix of the Multivariate Normal Simulations(Σ\SigmaΣ)¶

By substituting our derived short-rate volatility (σr(0)≈1.09545\sigma_{r(0)} \approx 1.09545σr(0)​≈1.09545) and our adjusted correlation (0.548) alongside standard historical estimates for Curvature (β2\beta_2β2​), we construct the finalized covariance matrix Σ\SigmaΣ that will feed directly into np.random.multivariate_normal


Σ=[1.00.5480.200.5481.200.3290.200.3291.0]\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 1.0 & 0.548 & 0.20 \\ 0.548 & 1.20 & 0.329 \\ 0.20 & 0.329 & 1.0 \end{bmatrix}Σ=⎣⎡​1.00.5480.20​0.5481.200.329​0.200.3291.0​⎦⎤​

💡 Crucial Scale Note: When we use this covariance matrix to calculate probabilities, a scale must be provided so that the probability calculations account for the elevated standard deviation of SOFR (1.09545 versus 1.0 for the Level (β0\beta_0β0​) and Curvature (β2\beta_2β2​).

🎲💧 Generate The Correlated Proabilities (Cumulative Densities)¶

First, we define our covariance matrix for the Level, SOFR, and Curvature. We will simulate a multivariate normal distribution and then convert those values into uniform probabilities using the Normal Cumulative Density Function (CDF).

# The uninformed covariance matrix (Level, SOFR, Curvature)
cov_matrix = np.array([
    [1.0,   0.548, 0.20],  # Level
    [0.548, 1.20,  0.329], # SOFR (Variance = 1.2)
    [0.20,  0.329, 1.0]    # Curvature
])

# Simulate the uninformed joint distribution
means = np.zeros(3)
num_sims = 10000
z_normal = np.random.multivariate_normal(means, cov_matrix, size=num_sims)

# Specific scales (standard deviations) so 1.0 is not assumed for all variables
# [sqrt(1.0), sqrt(1.2), sqrt(1.0)] equals [1.0, 1.09545, 1.0]
scales = np.sqrt(np.diag(cov_matrix))

# Generate the uniform probabilities using the exact scale vector
# NumPy automatically broadcasts 'scales' across the 3 columns of z_normal
corr_cdfs = norm.cdf(z_normal, loc=0, scale=scales)

# Verify the results for SOFR... it should go from 0 to 1
print(f"Column 1 (SOFR) Uniform Bounds: [{corr_cdfs[:, 1].min():.4f}, {corr_cdfs[:, 1].max():.4f}]")
Column 1 (SOFR) Uniform Bounds: [0.0001, 0.9999]

⚖️🎯 Convert the Correlated Probabiities to Correlated Tilted and Convicted Betas¶

Now, we take those correlated uniform probabilities (0 to 1) and pass them through the inverse Cumulative Distribution Function (beta.ppf) of our custom Beta distributions. The relative values of α\alphaα and β\betaβ determine the dynamic tilt (skew) and our specific conviction (spread) regarding current Fed actions..

🏛️ Scenario Setup: A Fed Cut Coincident with Quantitative Tightening (QT)¶

  • Level (β0\beta_0β0​): The long-term anchor. Set as symmetric (α=β=2.0\alpha = \beta = 2.0α=β=2.0) across a normal range from 5% to 7% (centered at 6%).

  • SOFR (r(0)): The monetary policy target. Tilted Left / Right-Skewed (α=2.0,β=6.0\alpha = 2.0, \beta = 6.0α=2.0,β=6.0), capping its range tightly between 3.0% and 3.6% to reflect a high-conviction projected Fed rate cut.

  • Curvature (β2\beta_2β2​):The bond supply factor. Tilted Right / Left-Skewed (α=6.0,β=2.0\alpha = 6.0, \beta = 2.0α=6.0,β=2.0), pushed higher to reflect the massive influx of term bond supply from Quantitative Tightening.

# The relative values of alpha and beta determine the tilt and conviction
a_params = np.array([2.0, 2.0, 6.0])  # Shape alpha
b_params = np.array([2.0, 6.0, 2.0])  # Shape beta

# Vectorize calculation of tilted and convicted beta distributions
standard_beta_matrix = beta.ppf(corr_cdfs, a_params, b_params)

# transforming our titled and convictedstandard [0, 1] beta samples into distributions.
# distribution of informed views = min + (max - min) * standard_sample

# LEVEL (Nelson-Siegel Beta_0): long-term rate expectation
# bounded between 5.00% and 7.00%
level = 0.05 + [0.07 - 0.05] * standard_beta_matrix[:, 0]

# SOFR (Nelson-Siegel Beta_1): anchored by SOFR
# bounded between 3.25% and 3.60% current Fed policy expectations
sofr = 0.0325 + [0.036 - 0.0325] * standard_beta_matrix[:, 1]

# CURVATURE (Nelson-Siegel Beta_2): "hump" of the curve
# allowed to swing from heavily inverted (-5%) to steeply humped (+5%)
curvature = -0.05 + [0.05 - (-0.05)] * standard_beta_matrix[:, 2]

Now that our standard [0,1][0, 1][0,1] beta outputs are translated into real-world interest rate boundaries, we can visualize the results. This final step reveals exactly how our specific macro views—injected via the skewness of the Beta distributions—reshape our simulated paths. By altering the shape parameters, we effectively force the simulation to “believe” that certain curve environments are more likely than others.

🎢🛡️ How Duration and Convexity Work With Realistic Risk¶

The work plan goes as follows:

  1. 📅 Define the Payoffs of Sample Bonds: The payment dates and amounts of the sample bonds are determined with the custom function create_payoff_df.

  2. 🧠📈 From Beliefs to Term Structures: Each of the 10,000 simulations is used to generate spot rates for each of the unique payments of the sample bonds. Those rates are converted to present value factors (or zero-coupon prices) and are used to calculate simulated bond prices. The ns_spot_rates function vectorizes the calculations across all 10,000 simulations for maximum efficiency.

  3. ⚖️🏆 Measuring the Success Of Strategies: The actual returns of a “duration-neutral” Barbell and Bullet strategy are calculated and compared under our simulated market conditions.

  4. 🔬 Visualizing The Results: The results are presented as return distributions.

2.1.a τ\tauτ Is The Volatility Spotlight 🔦¶

Our simulations will express the views of our Beta distributions, but exactly where do these views get expressed? τ\tauτ determines the expression of our views across the term structure. The impact of these views may be concentrated at short maturities, the mid-points (the belly), or out at the long end.To highlight how τ\tauτ affects the term structure, we’ll examine the value of the slope and curvature at different maturities using three different values for τ\tauτ:

  1. Base Case (τ\tauτ = 3.5829): The value estimated in a previous chapter with the May 15, 2026 data. The peak effect is just short of seven years.

  2. A τ\tauτ of 8.0: A value that delays the peak effect until slightly more than fourteen years.

  3. A τ\tauτ of 10.0: A value that pushes the peak effect out to almost eighteen years.

2.1.c Translating Simulated Rates to Zero-Coupon Prices 🏷️¶

Now that we have simulated our Nelson-Siegel parameters (Level, Slope, and Curvature) across 10,000 paths, we need to price our bonds. To do this, we first calculate the zero-coupon bond prices (often called discount factors) for every single cash flow date in our portfolios.

The SOFR Anchor in Action

Look closely at how we define the DataFrame columns in the code below. Because we anchored our short end to SOFR using the formula r(0)=Level+Sloper(0) = \text{Level} + \text{Slope}r(0)=Level+Slope, we can just back out the simulated slope by subtracting the Level from our SOFR simulation (sofr - level).Using the standard continuous compounding formula, the price of a zero-coupon bond is e−r⋅te^{-r \cdot t}e−r⋅t. We apply this math to all 10,000 simulated paths, looping it through our base case, the 8.0 transition case, and our 10.0 stress test.

Because df_payoff holds the exact cash flows (coupon and principal) for each bond, the Barbell and Bullet portfolios cash flows align to those dates. We’ll use a dot product (matrix multiplication) between these zero prices and df_payoff to get the final portfolio values.

# calculate the time to maturity (in years) for every cash flow date in our portfolios.
# our baseline date (May 15, 2026) is the settlement date to measure from.
mat_years = (pd.to_datetime(df_payoff.index) - pd.to_datetime('2026-05-15')).days / 365.25

# package our 10,000 simulated parameter paths into a single DataFrame.
# THE SOFR ANCHOR: Because we defined SOFR = Level + Slope, we can isolate
# the Slope parameter (beta_1) by simply subtracting Level from our simulated SOFR.
df = pd.DataFrame({
    'Level': level,
    'SOFR': sofr - level,  # This acts as our Slope parameter
    'Curvature': curvature
})

# define a helper function to calculate zero-coupon prices for any given tau
def rates_maturities(tau):
    # generate the simulated spot rates for all 10,000 paths using our Nelson-Siegel function
    rates, maturities = fq.ns_spot_rates(df, mat_years, fixed_tau=tau)

    # convert those yields into zero-coupon bond prices using continuous compounding: P = e^(-r*t)
    # we assign the exact cash flow dates back as column names so they line up with df_payoff
    return pd.DataFrame(np.exp(-rates * maturities), columns=df_payoff.index)

# generate the zero-coupon prices for our three "Volatility Spotlight" regimes
df_zeros_base_case = rates_maturities(3.5829)  # Base Case: Volatility hits the belly
df_zeros_8 = rates_maturities(8)               # Transition: Volatility moves outward
df_zeros_10 = rates_maturities(10)             # Stress Test: Volatility hits the long end

# inspect the resulting DataFrame for the base case

2.2.b Valuing the Portfolios: The Power of Aggregation 🧮¶

Instead of valuing each bond individually, we are going to aggregate the cash flows of our specific strategies first. Why? Because fundamentally, a portfolio of bonds is just one large stream of cash flows.

By summing the cash flows of the 1, 2, 3, 20, and 30-year bonds on each specific payment date, we create a single Barbell Payoff Vector. We do the same for the 5, 7, and 10-year bonds to create our Bullet Payoff Vector.

Once we have those aggregated cash flows, valuing the portfolio across all 10,000 simulations is as simple as taking the dot product of our simulated zero prices and our portfolio payoff vectors.

# define portfolio weights (normalized to 100% allocation across 8 available bonds)
# Barbell: equal 20% weighting in the wings (1Y, 2Y, 3Y and 20Y, 30Y bonds)
barbell_weights = np.array([0.2 if i in [0,1,2,6,7] else 0.0 for i in range(8)])

# Bullet: concentrated entirely in the belly (5Y, 7Y, 10Y bonds).
# these specific weights (19%, 23%, 58%) were meticulously chosen to ensure
# the Bullet has the exact same 7.27 year duration as the Barbell.
bullet_weights = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.19, 0.23, 0.58, 0.0, 0.0])

# calculate portfolio-level cash flow timelines
# multiplying the dataframe by the weights, we scale each bond's cash flows to its portfolio allocation.
# summing across axis=1 collapses the individual bonds into a single, unified cash flow vector.
barbell_payoffs = (df_payoff * barbell_weights).sum(axis=1)
bullet_payoffs = (df_payoff * bullet_weights).sum(axis=1)

# combine into a unified payoff DataFrame
# a clean timeline: Index = Payment Dates, Columns = Total Cash Flow for that strategy
df_portfolios_payoff = pd.DataFrame({
    'Barbell': barbell_payoffs,
    'Bullet': bullet_payoffs
})

2.2.b @ Multiplication Of Zero Prices and Portfolios’ Payoffs¶

Now comes the computationally beautiful part of our setup. We have two pieces of data ready to go:

  1. A massive matrix of zero-coupon prices (10,000 simulated paths ×\times× the number of payment dates).

  2. A single column of aggregated cash flows for each portfolio strategy.

To find the present value of our portfolios across all 10,000 alternate universes, we don’t need to write a slow, complicated for loop. Instead, we use Python’s @ operator, which is the dedicated symbol for matrix multiplication.

The @ operator takes each row of our simulated discount factors, multiplies them by the cash flows occurring on those corresponding dates, and sums them up to give us the total portfolio value. In a fraction of a second, this single line of code executes millions of individual discounting math problems, leaving us with 10,000 simulated portfolio values for both strategies.

## Valuing the Portfolios

# Assuming df_payoff has a 'Barbell' column and a 'Bullet' column
# representing the aggregated cash flows for each date

# Base Case (tau = 3.5829)
barbell_vals_base = df_zeros_base_case@(df_portfolios_payoff['Barbell'])
bullet_vals_base = df_zeros_base_case@(df_portfolios_payoff['Bullet'])

# Transition Case (tau = 8)
barbell_vals_8 = df_zeros_8@(df_portfolios_payoff['Barbell'])
bullet_vals_8 = df_zeros_8@(df_portfolios_payoff['Bullet'])

# Stress Test Case (tau = 10)
barbell_vals_10 = df_zeros_10@(df_portfolios_payoff['Barbell'])
bullet_vals_10 = df_zeros_10@(df_portfolios_payoff['Bullet'])

3 🏁 Calculating Simulated Rates of Return¶

Before we can calculate a return, we need a starting line. We assume the current prices of our portfolios are exactly equal to the present value of their cash flows, discounted using the actual empirical term structure from May 15, 2026.

We will first calculate this baseline “starting value” for both the Barbell and the Bullet. Once we have that price, calculating the simulated rates of return is just a matter of comparing our 10,000 simulated future portfolio values back to this single starting value.

## Calculating Today's Price and Simulated Returns

# 1. Define the exact May 15, 2026 empirical estimates
# Parameters are passed as: (Level, Slope, Curvature, Tau)
base_case = (0.0570, -0.0210, -0.016, 3.5829)

# 2. Calculate the base term structure
# This generates the exact spot rates for our cash flow timelines as of May 15, 2026
base_case_yields, maturity = fq.ns_spot_rates(base_case, mat_years)

# 3. Calculate today's exact portfolio prices using the @ operator
# First, we calculate the discount factors: e^(-r*t)
base_discount_factors = np.exp(-base_case_yields * maturity)

# Next, we multiply those discount factors by our aggregated portfolio payoffs
# to get today's starting value for both strategies
current_portfolio_values = base_discount_factors @ df_portfolios_payoff

current_barbell_price = current_portfolio_values['Barbell']
current_bullet_price = current_portfolio_values['Bullet']

# 4. Calculate the Simulated Rates of Return
# Return = (Simulated Value / Current Value) - 1
# base tau regime (tau = 3.5829) focus is 6.5 years
sim_returns_barbell_base = (barbell_vals_base / current_barbell_price) - 1
sim_returns_bullet_base = (bullet_vals_base / current_bullet_price) - 1

# moving the focus to 14 years

🚨 The Most Important Takeaway: The Illusion of the Single Number¶

If you take only one thing away from this simulation exercise, let it be this: never trust a single risk metric in a dynamic market.

Textbooks train you to look at a 7-year Barbell and a 7-year Bullet and see the exact same portfolio. But as our Copula simulations just proved, the moment the yield curve does anything other than a perfectly parallel shift, that illusion violently shatters.

  • Duration tells you what happens if the market behaves perfectly.

  • Convexity warns you that the price-yield relationship is curved, not straight.

  • Simulation forces you to face the reality that Level, Slope, and Curvature have a mind of their own.

Financial markets exist to transfer risk from those who don’t want it to those who know how to manage it. By replacing static assumptions with information-packed Beta distributions and historical correlation matrices, you are taking your first steps across that dividing line.
